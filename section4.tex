\section{Diagnostic Techniques}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The empty-chair frame produces interpretive structure (Section~\ref{sec:frame-as-method}) and a taxonomy of evidentiary claims (Section~\ref{sec:verification-regimes}). Across both, documentation, explanation, or approval may be treated as proof of a proposition its artifact does not expose. We call this structure the \emph{evidentiary-substitution sequence}; \emph{silence-manufacture} critically names instances in which unavailable contradictory evidence helps the substitution persist. The three techniques below expose the missing relation and the absent parties who depend on it.

The techniques can be used at several institutional moments. Architects can use
them to make evidentiary capabilities explicit; banks can use them to identify
compensating controls and procurement needs; examiners can use them to frame
requests within existing authority; and regulators can use recurring gaps to
inform policy. An affected party or advocate can identify the proposition at
stake, supply contradictory evidence, identify an unavailable object, or invoke
an existing complaint, appeal, correction, or review pathway. The ontology does
not itself create such a pathway, compel a response, shift a burden, or grant
access to protected evidence. Outputs differ because participants do not possess
the same authority, capability, access, or standing.

\subsection{Silence-manufacture as the underlying structural pattern}
\label{sec:diagnostics:silence-manufacture}

The paper joins several established accounts rather than displacing them.
Stakeholder theory directs attention to parties whose interests shape an
institution's responsibilities, while value-sensitive design asks how human
values enter technical design \parencite{freeman1984,friedman1996}. Agency
theory explains why actors with different interests and information incur
monitoring and bonding costs \parencite{jensenMeckling1976}. Organizational
decoupling and audit scholarship explain how formal structures and verification
rituals can diverge from operational effect
\parencite{meyer1977,power1997,strathern1997}. These traditions already explain
affected interests, costly monitoring, information asymmetry, and ceremonial
substitution.

Assurance cases already organize claims, supporting argument and evidence, and
their context or assumptions \parencite{bloomfield2010}. This paper does not
discover claims, warrants, or defeaters; it connects assurance reasoning to
affected interests, uneven authority and capability, and selected FS~AI~RMF
controls where contradictory evidence may be difficult to observe.

The narrower contribution here is to connect those concerns through an
artifact-to-inference sequence in the FS~AI~RMF setting: an affected interest
is translated into a control objective and required evidentiary object; an
available artifact is asked to support an inference; a claimed binding is
tested; and the gap is assigned to an actor with authority, capability,
obligation, or standing to act. This coordination ontology makes it possible to
distinguish a current examination question from a policy recommendation and a
vendor capability from a bank obligation. \emph{Evidentiary-substitution
sequence} is the neutral operational term; \emph{silence-manufacture} is the
critical term where unavailable contradictory evidence helps a substitution
persist. Neither label alone establishes intention, causation, violation, or
legal significance, or covers every information asymmetry or documentation
failure.

\subsubsection{Definition}
\label{sec:diagnostics:silence-manufacture:definition}

We define the \textbf{evidentiary-substitution sequence} as follows: a claim requires an evidentiary object that is unavailable; a different artifact is exposed; a broader inference is claimed from it without an inspectable binding; contradictory evidence is unavailable; and an affected party bears the resulting gap. We use \textbf{silence-manufacture} as the critical name when the unavailability of contradictory evidence helps that substitution persist. A metric, document, signature, or explanation becomes a substitute only when it is asked to warrant a proposition beyond the object it exposes.

Silence is the \emph{enabling condition}; substitution is the \emph{active mechanism}. The artifact is real. A SHAP value is a real numerical computation. An audit document is a real institutional production. A signed adverse action notice is a real legal instrument. Each can support claims appropriate to it. The sequence begins when an institution treats the artifact as evidence of a different proposition without making the relation inspectable, while system or process conditions leave affected parties unable to produce the evidence that would test that relation. The concept therefore criticizes neither documentation nor explanation as such. It identifies a specific inference gap; the causal claim remains separately testable.

\subsubsection{Relationship to Goodhart's law}
\label{sec:diagnostics:silence-manufacture:goodhart}

Silence-manufacture is a complementary pattern to Goodhart's law \parencite{goodhart1984problems}. The comparison is an orientation device, not a claim of mathematical duality or an extension of Goodhart's result.

Goodhart's law describes optimization pressure on a metric corrupting the metric: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The pressure lands on the visible; the visible breaks. Silence-manufacture describes suppression of evidence needed to test the inference drawn from a visible artifact: silence is treated as confirmation even though the architecture helps produce it. The pressure lands on what cannot readily be observed; the artifact appears adequate because the evidence needed to contest its broader use is missing.

Goodhart describes corruption of metrics; silence-manufacture describes an evidentiary substitution that can leave a metric intact. A regulator may improve a visible measure while leaving unchanged the conditions that prevent an affected party from testing what the measure is taken to establish. We return to this possibility below as a proposition generated by the frame, not as a universal law of reform.

\subsubsection{Relationship to decoupling and the audit society}
\label{sec:diagnostics:silence-manufacture:decoupling}

Meyer and Rowan's account of decoupling describes formal structures maintained
apart from operational activity; Power's audit society and Strathern's account
of audit culture describe verification practices that can displace the
substance they were meant to secure. The broad phenomenon of an artifact
standing in for activity is therefore not new, and we do not claim it is.

The narrower contribution here is a diagnostic sequence. What evidentiary
object does a claim require? What feature of law, architecture, interface,
method, data, or cost makes that object unavailable? What artifact is offered
instead, what inference is drawn from it, and which absent party depends on the
inference? This sequence does not presume organizational bad faith or even a
coupled alternative that was feasible. It can therefore distinguish ceremonial
decoupling from cases in which the relevant silence is legally required,
architecturally produced, or methodologically unavoidable.

\subsubsection{Mapping the three earned positions onto silence-manufacture}
\label{sec:diagnostics:silence-manufacture:mapping-positions}

The three positions expose the same evidentiary sequence at different levels,
but they do not depend on identical technical mechanisms. Position~1
(governance versus governance theater) is the enclosing institutional frame;
Positions~2 and~3 identify particular explanation and accountability failures
within it. Their common structure is the inference from an exposed artifact to
a proposition that the artifact, without an inspectable binding, does not
establish.

\emph{Position 2: explanation artifacts do not verify evidentiary objects they
do not expose.} The relevant limitation depends on the method. SHAP attributes
a prediction under a specified feature-coalition formulation; LIME fits a local
surrogate around an instance \parencite{lundberg2017,ribeiro2016}. Neither is,
without an additional warrant, a transcript of internal computation or a human
decision process. Attention weights have likewise been shown not necessarily to
support the causal interpretations assigned to them \parencite{jain2019}.
Chain-of-thought studies provide a different kind of evidence: generated traces
can omit influential cues or vary independently of answers in studied settings
\parencite{lanham2023,turpin2023}. Slack et al. show that post-hoc methods can
also be manipulated to conceal biased classifier behavior \parencite{slack2020}.
These results do not make the methods interchangeable or useless. They show why
the proposition asserted must be matched to the artifact actually produced.

\emph{Position 3: review of an explanation is not verification of an unexposed
decision process.} A signature can establish authorization, completion, and the
identity of a reviewer. It supports a stronger claim only when the reviewer had
access to evidence capable of testing that claim and the record shows what was
tested. Research on sycophancy and evaluator-facing optimization illustrates
why approval signals can diverge from the qualities they are taken to measure
\parencite{sharma2023,perez2023}; it does not establish that every approval does
so. The regulator's question is therefore about reviewer access, competence,
and recorded basis, not the mere presence or absence of a signature.

\emph{Position 1: governance documentation is evidence of documented
commitments and activities, not automatically of operational effect.} Policies,
inventories, dashboards, and audit records can be indispensable governance
artifacts. The inference gap appears when their existence is treated as proof
that deployed behavior conforms to them, although the relevant operations were
not independently observed or tested. Here again, the remedy is not less
documentation but a declared relation between the artifact, the proposition,
and the evidence capable of defeating the proposition.

The unification is diagnostic rather than causal. Across the three levels, an
examiner can identify the proposition at issue, the available artifact, the
claimed binding, and the evidence that could disconfirm it. That common sequence
is useful even when the causes of unavailability and the appropriate remedies
differ.

\subsubsection{What silence-manufacture predicts}
\label{sec:diagnostics:silence-manufacture:predictions}

The frame suggests three propositions that distinguish it from a generic
Goodhart-shaped critique. We state them with observations that would count
against them.

\emph{Proposition 1: metric-layer reform can leave an inference gap intact.}
Tighter thresholds, controls, or documentation requirements can improve a
visible artifact without making independently testable the broader proposition
for which it is used. The proposition would be weakened where metric-only
reforms reliably make that broader proposition independently testable, without
changes to evidence capture, access, or binding. Section~\ref{sec:primitives}
develops architectural responses; Section~\ref{sec:uncertainties} marks the
political-economy assumptions on which adoption depends.

\emph{Proposition 2: inquiry can partition around unavailable evidence.}
Observers with different access may form durable, internally coherent accounts
when evidence capable of resolving their disagreement is unavailable. The
proposition would be weakened if the disagreement persisted after the relevant
evidence became mutually available and its provenance could be tested. More
research is not necessarily curative; the diagnostic question is whether the
researchers are able to observe the object over which they disagree.

\emph{Proposition 3: public narratives can inherit the shape of unavailable
evidence.} The BSA/AML prohibition on disclosing suspicious-activity reports
creates a concrete setting in which customers and outside observers may lack
access to an institution's account of a closure \parencite{usc31_5318g2}.
A Reuters complaint review reported by Anthony --- 35 of 8,361
account-closure complaints mentioned political or religious motivations ---
illustrates the need to distinguish a salient narrative from the observed
complaint distribution \parencite{anthony2026}; it does not prove what caused
every closure or every narrative. The proposition would be weakened if access to the previously
unavailable evidence did not materially change the explanations formed by
affected parties or public observers.

These are research propositions, not results established by this position
paper. Their value is that they identify observations capable of refining or
defeating the frame at three sites: regulatory reform, analytical inquiry, and
public discourse.

\subsection{Three diagnostic techniques}
\label{sec:diagnostics:three-techniques}

The techniques have two layers. An \emph{orienting question} locates a possible
gap; it is not an examination procedure or finding. Where established authority
permits inquiry, a structured evidence request can then test a proposition. The
same structure also helps a bank formulate procurement needs, a vendor specify
an export, or a regulator identify a recurring policy gap.

\subsubsection{Technique 1: Empty-chair enumeration}
\label{sec:diagnostics:enumeration}

The orienting question is: \emph{which affected interest depends on this
artifact, and what evidence would show that the implementation represents that
interest?} The output is a mapping among affected parties, control objectives,
evidentiary objects, and the capabilities required to expose them. Conflicting
interests remain visible rather than being collapsed into a generic
``stakeholder'' field.

For a denied applicant, for example, understandability, method fidelity,
policy conformity, and access to contest are distinct propositions. An
architect can select capabilities against those propositions; a bank can state
which are available or compensating; and an examiner can test only those claims
within applicable authority.

The mapping is useful precisely when participants disagree. A bank may identify
the future compliance officer and the examiner as the principal users of an AI
inventory, while an affected customer may depend on the inventory only as an
index to case-level evidence. That does not make the inventory useless to the
customer or sufficient for the customer's claim. It reveals a chain: the
inventory represents organizational accountability directly and supports later
retrieval indirectly. The mapping should therefore record both the interest and
the proposition, the available artifact, the missing object, and the actor who
could supply it. A list of stakeholders without those relations preserves names
while losing the governance problem.

Enumeration also exposes conflicts. Detailed evidence access may improve
contestability while creating privacy, cybersecurity, suspicious-activity
report confidentiality, or intellectual-property risks. The technique does not
rank those interests automatically. It requires the institution to identify the
conflict, applicable authority, access boundary, and available alternative---for
example, protected examiner access, independent validation, aggregation, or a
reasoned disclosure that the proposition cannot be tested by the affected party.

\subsubsection{Technique 2: Pages missing from the ledger}
\label{sec:diagnostics:pages-missing}

``Pages missing from the ledger'' remains a metaphor for record coverage, not a
claim that every event should be attested. Its orienting question is: \emph{which
categories lack contemporaneous evidence, relative to what declared scope, and
what pattern results?} The operational denominator is a declared attestation
scope: the decision universe; category definitions and inclusion or exclusion
rules; the rationale and version history for those boundaries; operational
counts and risk weighting; reconciliation to system inventories; and both
institution-selected and examiner-selected samples.

This denominator matters because raw attestation volume is gameable. An
institution could document many low-risk cases while consequential categories
remain outside the declared or operational scope. Comparison with operational
populations, high-risk categories, boundary changes, inventory records, and
externally selected samples makes such curation more expensive. A discrepancy
is evidence about coverage. It does not, without additional evidence, prove a
motive, violation, or produced-absence mechanism.

Declared scope also needs revision history. If a bank changes a category
boundary after adverse cases arise, the new boundary may be reasonable, but a
current manifest alone cannot show how the earlier population was treated.
Counts before and after the change, the rationale, approval, and reconciliation
to operational systems let a reviewer distinguish an ordinary taxonomy repair
from unexplained loss of coverage. Risk weighting prevents another collapse:
one unattested high-consequence category may matter more than thousands of
routine attested events. Institution-selected samples can demonstrate that a
control operates in expected cases; examiner-selected or otherwise external
samples test whether the demonstration survives selection outside the
institution's curated set.

The output is not a mythical complete ledger. It is an inspectable account of
the denominator and its limits. Some categories may remain unattested because
capture would be disproportionate, legally restricted, technically unavailable,
or dependent on a vendor. Recording that fact and its consequence lets a bank
adopt a compensating process or narrow its claim, lets an examiner assess what
the existing record warrants, and lets a regulator see whether the same missing
capability recurs across institutions.

\subsubsection{Technique 3: The produced-silence question pair}
\label{sec:diagnostics:question-pair}

The orienting question is: \emph{what access or observability changed, what
artifact is offered in its place, and which actor draws what inference from
it?} A post-decision justification regime, generic notice, or challenge channel
may raise the question. Classification as produced absence still requires the
comparative, counterfactual, provenance, or user evidence described in
Section~\ref{sec:frame-as-method}. Later reflection can add genuine reasoning;
the diagnostic asks that it be identified as a later evidentiary object rather
than silently substituted for a decision-time record.

The question pair should identify a mechanism and an inference separately. A
high complaint cost may reduce observable complaints, but no silence-manufacture
sequence exists unless an actor uses the complaint record to support a broader
claim. Conversely, an institution may draw an overbroad inference from a sparse
record without having caused the sparsity. That is still an artifact-to-inference
gap, but not yet produced absence. Separating the two claims prevents the vivid
language of suppression from doing empirical work the evidence has not done.

Useful tests follow from the alleged mechanism. If channel friction is the
cause, challenge rates or the composition of challengers should change when
friction materially changes. If record design overwrites earlier states,
version provenance should reveal the loss or an independent source should
preserve a different history. If selection is the issue, external sampling or a
newly observable population should alter the assessed proposition. Stable
behavior, representative records, or unchanged conclusions after access expands
count against the produced-absence account. A finding, if any, must still rest
on applicable authority and the evidence obtained, not on the orienting label.

\subsection{From orientation to evidence request}
\label{sec:diagnostics:evidence-request}

Each of the three techniques ends in the same place: a question about a
specific artifact, a specific proposition, and the relation between them.
That question becomes usable when it is written as a request a recipient can
answer, refuse, or answer in part with a stated reason.
Section~\ref{sec:request} gives that form and works one FS~AI~RMF control
objective through it. The remainder of this section concerns what happens when
such requests are not made.

\subsection{Coordination consequence}
\label{sec:diagnostics:compounding}

The three techniques compound without becoming a checklist. Enumeration says
whose interest supplies the purpose; declared scope exposes where records do
and do not cover the operational population; and the produced-silence question
tests whether the pattern may be endogenous to the process being evaluated.
Orientation may lead to an evidence request; evidence may support or defeat an
institutional inference; only applicable law or guidance supplies the basis for
a formal finding. Unresolved recurring gaps can instead become procurement
requirements, vendor design problems, or regulatory policy questions.

Section~\ref{sec:primitives} proposes evidentiary capabilities that could make
these requests answerable. Their absence is not automatically a violation, and
their presence is not proof that the corresponding governance claim is true.
